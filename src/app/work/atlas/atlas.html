Atlas

Port terminals run on precision. The tools planners used to map them did not. Atlas was built to close that gap.

Atlas is a purpose-built port terminal mapping tool designed for logistics planners. Sitting at the crossroads of the visual familiarity of Photoshop and the spatial intelligence of Google Maps, it gives terminal planners a dedicated environment to map port infrastructure using terminal-specific objects that generate coordinates directly usable by downstream planning systems. What once took 200 to 400 hours per terminal now takes a fraction of the time — with no compromise on accuracy.

Business Analysis Interaction Design Supply Chain & Logistics

The Problem

Port terminal logistics is a world of precision where every container, crane, and corridor needs to be accounted for before a single plan can be made. The stakes are high and the margins for error are thin. Yet despite the complexity of the work, the tools available to terminal planners were never built with them in mind.

The existing workflow relied on a 3D mapping tool, powerful in theory, but deeply misaligned with how terminal planners actually worked. Objects had to be placed with precision, coordinates had to be generated accurately, and any error meant going back. It was a process as tedious as it was time-consuming, leaving teams with little room for agility, iteration, or scale.

The Design

At the heart of Atlas is a library of terminal-specific objects — cranes, containers, corridors, berths, and the full vocabulary of port infrastructure — that planners can place, arrange, and configure to build an accurate digital representation of their terminal. Each placed object generates coordinates directly usable by downstream planning systems, removing the manual translation step that had previously added time and introduced error.

The interface was designed around the planner’s workflow, not the other way around. Layouts were kept clean and purposeful, tools were surfaced contextually, and the mapping process was structured to mirror how planners actually think about their terminals — spatially, operationally, and iteratively.

Atlas reduced terminal mapping time by over 75%.

The Outcome

Atlas turned a process that once consumed weeks of effort into one that could be completed in a fraction of the time, without sacrificing accuracy or detail. Planners who had worked around the limitations of the old tool found themselves able to map, iterate, and update terminals at a pace that matched the speed of real port operations.

More than a time saving, Atlas gave terminal planners something the previous tools never could — a sense of ownership over their own maps, and the confidence that what they were building was accurate, usable, and built for them.
